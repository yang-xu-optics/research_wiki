# HHG entanglement mechanism

> Entanglement in HHG appears when the optical field is quantized and the same electronic/matter dynamics coherently mediates photon exchange among the fundamental driver and many harmonic modes.

## Origin

The semiclassical HHG picture treats the strong laser as a fixed waveform and computes a classical emitted harmonic field. That approximation hides field-state information. Quantum-optical HHG instead keeps the driver and harmonic modes as quantum systems, which makes mode correlations, squeezing, and measurement backaction visible — [[quantum-optical-nature-hhg-gorlach]], [[strong-field-quantum-optics]].

## How it works

HHG is an extreme frequency-conversion process: many photons from the driver are converted through a material dipole/current into fewer high-energy harmonic photons. If every emission event were independent and the material response were just a deterministic classical current, the output modes could remain approximately independent coherent states.

Entanglement appears when the matter response has quantum correlations. In atomic HHG, strong driving can deplete the ground state and create time-dependent dipole-moment correlations. In solid-state HHG, electron-hole, current, excitonic, or topological correlations can play an analogous role. Because all harmonic modes are coupled to the same quantum matter response, the emitted modes no longer factorize into independent states — [[entanglement-squeezing-optical-field-modes-hhg-stammer]], [[solid-state-high-harmonic-generation]].

Operationally, this shows up as two-mode or multimode squeezing, nonclassical intensity correlations, Cauchy-Schwarz inequality violation, or conditional state changes under measurement — [[evidence-quantum-optical-nature-hhg-theidel]], [[entanglement-measurement-theory-hhg-stammer]].

## Why ground-state depletion matters

In the weak-depletion limit, the atom or emitter is approximately reset to the same ground state after each infinitesimal emission event. Then the dipole at time t behaves almost like a deterministic source term, and two-time dipole averages nearly factorize:

`<d(t)d(t')> ~= <d(t)><d(t')>`.

That factorization is what lets the emitted modes look like independent coherent states.

When the ground state is depleted, the first interaction changes the later emitter state. Emission or ionization at time t changes the population and coherence available at time t'. The dipole operator at different times then has connected correlations:

`<d(t)d(t')> - <d(t)><d(t')> != 0`.

Those connected dipole correlations are the microscopic channel that couples field modes. Since different harmonic modes are all radiated by the same time-dependent dipole/current, a fluctuation in one emitted mode carries information about fluctuations in the others, producing squeezing and entanglement — [[entanglement-squeezing-optical-field-modes-hhg-stammer]], [[quantum-optics-optical-coherence-hhg-stammer]].

## Trade-offs / Contrasts with

- vs semiclassical HHG — the harmonic spectrum may look classical, while higher-order correlations reveal entanglement.
- vs perturbative SPDC/SFWM — HHG is nonperturbative and can access XUV/attosecond bands, but the same strong dynamics makes state preservation and tomography harder.
- vs [[quantum-light-driven-hhg]] — entanglement can be generated by HHG even from a coherent driver; quantum-light drivers add extra input correlations and control knobs.

## Open questions

- Which entanglement witness is most experimentally robust for broadband HHG: two-mode squeezing, Cauchy-Schwarz violation, g(2), or conditional tomography?
- Can the material system be engineered so the entangled modes are spectrally clean and useful for quantum technology?
