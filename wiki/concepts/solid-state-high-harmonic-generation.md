# Solid-state high-harmonic generation

> Solid-state HHG uses crystals, correlated materials, semiconductors, or topological systems as the HHG medium, turning high harmonics into probes and possible generators of ultrafast quantum-optical material response.

## Origin

The review by Ciappina frames solid-state HHG as a bridge between ultrafast spectroscopy, quantum optics, and condensed matter — [[solid-state-high-order-harmonic-generation-frontiers-ciappina]]. The newer papers add quantum-light drivers, topological signatures, excitonic enhancement, and edge-state physics — [[crystal-hhg-driven-by-quantum-light-gothelf]], [[band-topology-quantum-optical-signatures-hhg-ilin]], [[edge-states-quantum-optical-hhg-lange]].

## How it works

Instead of atomic ionization and recombination, solid-state HHG is often described through interband polarization, intraband currents, electron-hole motion, Berry curvature, topology, excitons, and dephasing. Quantum-optical treatments ask how those material correlations show up as squeezing, photon statistics, or mode correlations in emitted harmonics.

## Trade-offs / Contrasts with

- vs gas HHG — solids offer compactness, high density, and material-specific spectroscopy; gases offer cleaner recollision physics and less damage/dephasing complexity.
- vs [[iii-v-integrated-photonics]] — solid HHG may eventually connect to chip-scale materials, but current HHG field strengths and damage constraints are much harsher.
- vs [[silicon-nitride]]/AlGaAs nonlinear optics — HHG is more extreme and broadband, but less mature as an integrated photonics function.

## Open questions

- Can quantum-optical HHG signatures become practical probes of topology, excitons, and correlations?
- Which nanostructures or photonic platforms can enhance HHG while preserving quantum-optical information?
